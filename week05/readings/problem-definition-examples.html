Problem Definition Examples

Turn a broad business concern into an answerable analysis question

Begin with the decision, not the chart

A data project is useful when it helps a stakeholder make a decision. A broad concern such as “traffic safety is getting worse” may motivate the work, but it is not yet an analysis question.

Use this chain:

Symptom or concern -> stakeholder and decision -> focused question -> measurable objectives -> evidence and limitations

A framing worksheet

Answer these questions before writing code:

  1. What symptom, opportunity, or decision created the request?
  2. Who will use the result, and what can that person or organization change?
  3. What population, geography, and time period are in scope?
  4. What outcome matters, and how is it represented in the data?
  5. What explanatory or segmentation variables are available?
  6. What resources, deadlines, and ethical constraints limit the project?
  7. What related work or prior evidence should inform the analysis?
  8. What would count as an actionable result?
TipA useful scope test

A strong question is simultaneously interesting, actionable, and answerable with the available data. Anything outside that overlap belongs in a limitation or a future-data recommendation.

Example: traffic crashes

Too broad: Why do crashes happen?

Better decision frame: A transportation safety team must decide which conditions and locations deserve targeted investigation.

Focused question: Among reported Chicago crashes from 2018 through 2025, which combinations of roadway conditions, weather, location, and time are associated with severe outcomes?

Possible objectives:

  • Describe crash counts and severity rates by year, season, and time of day.
  • Compare severe-outcome percentages across selected roadway and weather conditions.
  • Identify geographic areas with unusually high severe-crash counts after considering traffic volume if a suitable denominator is available.
  • Recommend two conditions or locations for deeper investigation.

Boundary: Crash records can show reported associations. They do not by themselves prove that a condition caused a crash or measure risk without an exposure denominator.

Example: short-term rentals

Too broad: What affects Airbnb prices?

Better decision frame: A tourism analyst wants to understand how the mix and pricing of listings differs across markets or neighborhoods.

Focused question: How do listed price and future availability differ by room type, capacity, and geography in the selected market snapshot?

Possible objectives:

  • Compare median listed price and IQR across room types.
  • Estimate how price changes with guest capacity while acknowledging other listing differences.
  • Describe the geographic distribution of listing types.
  • Identify segments that warrant a more detailed pricing analysis.

Boundary: Listed price is not necessarily the price paid. An unavailable calendar date is not necessarily a booked stay.

Example: appointments and satisfaction

Too broad: How can the clinic improve?

Better decision frame: A clinic manager needs to decide where a limited scheduling-improvement effort should begin.

Focused question: Which appointment characteristics are associated with no-shows, and how do satisfaction measures differ across operational segments in the synthetic, de-identified EHR data?

Possible objectives:

  • Measure no-show rates by appointment type, lead time, and time of day.
  • Compare satisfaction distributions across selected service groups.
  • Identify segments with both operational volume and unfavorable outcomes.
  • Recommend a pilot intervention and state what would be measured after implementation.

Boundary: The course EHR data supports analysis practice, not clinical decisions. Associations must not be interpreted as patient blame or medical causation.

Use specific objective verbs

Prefer verbs that make the expected result visible.

Purpose Useful verbs
Summarize Describe, measure, estimate, categorize
Compare Compare, rank, assess
Explore relationships Identify, examine, explore
Anticipate Forecast, predict
Support action Recommend, prioritize

Avoid using only “analyze” or “understand.” Those words do not tell the reader what evidence you will produce.

Turn expectations into hypotheses

A hypothesis is a specific, testable expectation. It should name variables and a direction or benchmark when a defensible direction exists.

Relationship

Listings that accommodate more guests tend to have higher listed prices.

Difference between groups

The severe-crash percentage differs between daylight and darkness conditions.

Comparison with a benchmark

The no-show rate for appointments scheduled more than 30 days in advance exceeds the overall no-show rate.

These hypotheses concern variables. “Chicago will differ from Milwaukee” is incomplete until you say which measured outcome will differ and how.

Check the finished problem statement

  • Is the stakeholder named?
  • Is the decision or action clear?
  • Are the population, time, and geography bounded?
  • Is the outcome measurable with the available data?
  • Do the objectives collectively answer the question?
  • Are causal words avoided unless the design supports causality?
  • Are privacy, missingness, selection, and measurement risks acknowledged?
