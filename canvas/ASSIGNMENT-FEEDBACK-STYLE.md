# Assignment feedback in the instructor's voice

Use this guide when drafting grading comments, rubric feedback, or revision
notes. It is based on recurring patterns in the instructor's local Canvas ops
export, `all_my_canvas_comments.txt`. Keep that private export outside public
course materials. Do not copy student names, IDs, grades, group details, or
identifiable submission passages into tracked files.

These seeds are edited, generalized examples, not verbatim historical comments
or new grading decisions. Adapt them to the current submission and rubric.

## Voice

- Start with the work: the answer, chart, hypothesis, output, or interpretation.
  Skip introductory summaries of the assignment.
- Be direct and conversational. Use "you," "your," and contractions. Use "I"
  when it describes an actual observation or an instructor expectation.
- One sentence is enough for a missing item. A conceptual correction usually
  needs two to four: what is happening, why it matters, what to do next.
  Longer project feedback can follow the actual rubric sections.
- Brief praise is fine when earned: "Looks good," "Nice work," or "No major
  issues here." Prefer a specific observation. Do not force a positive opening
  or closing onto every correction.
- Distinguish required fixes ("Include...", "Complete...") from suggestions
  ("You might want to..."). Do not turn optional advice into a deduction.
- Explain analysis in practical terms: what the audience should learn and
  what recommendation the evidence supports.
- Name the actual question, variable, chart, or hypothesis. Use step numbers
  only when they match the current assignment.
- Preserve directness, not transcription artifacts. Clean up repeated "you
  know," false starts, software-name errors, encoding damage, and run-ons.
  Do not manufacture typos to sound human.

## Comment seeds

Replace bracketed details with verified evidence, not assumptions.

### Missing work and file formats

"Include answers for questions [numbers]."

"The summary is included, but the [required workbook/source file] is missing.
Include that file so I can follow how you created the analysis."

"Save the workbook as .xlsx. A .csv file won't retain the pivot tables or
charts, and I don't want you to lose that work."

### Correct work and next steps

"Your [specific analysis] looks good. Include the chart next to the
interpretation so the reader can follow both in one place."

"No major issues here. You might want to look at [specific comparison] next
to see [question supported by this analysis]."

### Summary statistics and cross-tabs

"For the cross-tab, start with counts and calculate row or column percentages
from those counts. Percentages of averages won't show the share in each group."

"The mean and median are different here. Look at the distribution to see
whether a few large values are pulling the mean up. What would you check next?"

### Charts

"Use a histogram to show the distribution of [continuous variable]. That will
make it easier to see the shape and whether there are outliers."

"Check the aggregation on [chart]. Adding up [rate or percentage] doesn't
answer this question. Use [appropriate summary or individual observations]
so the chart shows [intended comparison]."

"The chart needs [axis labels/units/a legend]. A reader should be able to tell
what it shows without an oral explanation."

### Hypotheses and planning

"Be more specific about what you expect to find. Which groups are you
comparing, which variable are you measuring, and what difference do you expect?"

"Define what you mean by [vague term]. You need a measure or a rule for
classifying it before you can test the relationship."

"The headline and the relationships are running together here. State the main
idea first, then list the relationships you would test to support it."

### Statistical and practical interpretation

"Compare the p-value with alpha and state whether you reject or fail to reject
the null hypothesis. Then explain that result using your variables."

"More detail on the practical interpretation. Why is this important, and what
recommendation would you make for [relevant audience]?"

"Bring the effect size into the interpretation. Statistical significance
doesn't tell the reader how large or useful the difference is."

"The overall chi-square result tells you there is an association. Look at the
residuals to explain which categories contribute to that result."

### Code and reproducibility

"Check that the setup chunk has run and the data and required packages are
loaded. Then run the example chunk to narrow down where the problem starts."

"Check the variable name against the data. R is case-sensitive, so [actual
name] and [name in the submission] are different."

"Include the steps you used in [tool], not just the final output. I should be
able to follow your instructions and recreate the analysis."

### Reports and presentations

"The key takeaway is hard to follow without the presentation. State the
finding directly and explain what you recommend doing with it."

"Use the same colors for the same groups across charts. That will make the
comparisons easier to follow."

"Too much text on [specific slide]. Keep the main point on the slide and move
the supporting detail into the oral explanation."

### Revision and help

"Complete [specific missing part]. Reach out with questions."

Only add "resubmit," a deadline, a promise to regrade, or an invitation to
email when current policy or instructor direction authorizes it.

## Avoid generic AI-style feedback

- No automatic "Great job!" or identical praise/correction/praise structure.
- Avoid inflated praise such as "exceptional insight," "masterful analysis,"
  or "a comprehensive understanding" when a specific observation fits.
- Avoid stock transitions and abstractions: "Furthermore," "Moreover,"
  "to elevate your work," "delve deeper," "leverage," "robust analysis," or
  "enhance clarity" without saying exactly what needs to change.
- Do not restate the submission, repeat the rubric, add a concluding recap,
  or create headings for a two-sentence comment.
- Prefer ordinary periods and commas. Skip decorative em dashes, bold labels,
  and elaborate formatting in short Canvas comments.
- Do not just swap synonyms to make a batch look varied. Let the evidence and
  issue determine wording and length. Reusing a concise correction is fine
  when students make the same mistake.
- These preferences guide our feedback. They are not evidence that student
  writing was AI-generated.

## Evidence and grading boundaries

- Read the current prompt, rubric, and submission before selecting a seed.
  Historical homework numbers and grading rules may no longer match.
- Do not invent missing work, results, deductions, or reasons for an error.
  "Code runs properly" requires an actual run; seeing output is not equivalent.
- Never claim "I fixed the code," "output is attached," or "updated your
  project" unless that action occurred in the current task.
- Some historical comments authorized hypothetical results for specific
  practice exercises when code failed. That is not a standing rule. Never
  invent results or tell students to invent them unless the current exercise
  explicitly permits clearly labeled hypothetical results.
- Feedback must agree with the recorded rubric assessment. These seeds do not
  authorize grade changes or introduce new criteria.
- Drafting feedback does not authorize posting it. Use the existing reviewed
  grading/upload workflow and follow the user's posting instructions.

Before using a comment, check: Is it supported by this submission? Does it
match the current rubric? Does the student know what to do next? Can filler
be removed? Are claims about code runs, edits, and attachments accurate?
