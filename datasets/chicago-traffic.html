Chicago Traffic Crashes

Crashes, vehicles, people, and numerical weather, 2018–2025

A relational release of Chicago police-reported crashes with NOAA weather enrichment
Note

Release: project1/2026-fall/v1/traffic
Coverage: January 1, 2018 through December 31, 2025
Geography: City of Chicago
License: City of Chicago Data Portal terms; NOAA observations are U.S. government data

About this dataset

This release combines Chicago’s police-reported crash, vehicle, and person records with a one-row-per-crash analysis table containing calendar fields, geographic keys, and the nearest qualifying NOAA observation from O’Hare or Midway. It is useful for questions about crash frequency, injury severity, contributing factors, vehicle and driver characteristics, weather, time, and place.

The base files deliberately do not contain Census demographics or other ready-made enrichments. Students should choose compatible external data, justify the geography and vintage, and validate the resulting join.

Files

File Rows One row represents Primary key
chicago_traffic_crashes.parquet 877,920 One crash crash_record_id
chicago_traffic_vehicles.parquet 1,792,368 One crash unit, usually a vehicle, pedestrian, or cyclist crash_unit_id
chicago_traffic_people.parquet 1,927,651 One person represented in a crash report person_id
chicago_traffic_crash_analysis.parquet 877,920 One crash with time, geography, and numeric weather crash_record_id

How the files relate

From To Join key Relationship
Crashes Crash analysis crash_record_id One-to-one
Crashes Vehicles crash_record_id One-to-many
Crashes People crash_record_id One-to-many
Vehicles People vehicle_id One-to-many when the person is linked to a unit

Do not join vehicles and people directly to crashes and then total crash-level measures. A crash with three units and four people can become as many as twelve rows. Summarize the many-side table first unless person- or unit-level records are the intended result.

Useful geographic fields include state_fips, city_geoid, zip5, police_beat, latitude, and longitude. ZIP codes are routing regions rather than Census geographies; for demographic analysis, a documented spatial join to a Census geography is generally stronger.

Example: crash-level vehicle counts

library(arrow)
library(dplyr)

base_url <- "https://data.60land.com/project1/2026-fall/v1/traffic/"

crashes <- read_parquet(paste0(base_url, "chicago_traffic_crashes.parquet"))
vehicles <- read_parquet(paste0(base_url, "chicago_traffic_vehicles.parquet"))

vehicle_summary <- vehicles |>
  count(crash_record_id, name = "vehicle_rows")

crash_level <- crashes |>
  left_join(vehicle_summary, by = "crash_record_id")

Important limitations

  • These are police-reported crashes, not every collision or traffic hazard.
  • reported_weather_condition is an officer-reported category. NOAA fields in the analysis table are numerical observations from an airport station and may not reproduce conditions at the exact crash location.
  • Crash timestamps are converted to Central Standard Time before selecting the closest NOAA observation within 90 minutes because NOAA LCD does not use daylight saving time.
  • Coordinates are source coordinates. Missing values and source 0,0 placeholders are represented as missing.
  • Officer-assigned contributing causes should not be interpreted as experimental evidence of causation.

Provenance and privacy

Source records come from the City of Chicago Traffic Crashes–Crashes, Vehicles, and People datasets, the City’s ZIP boundary lookup, and NOAA Local Climatological Data. Driver-license fields, home geography, hospital/EMS identifiers, and selected carrier, permit, towing, and hazardous-material identifiers are excluded from the public course release.
