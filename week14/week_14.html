Week 14 - Ethics in Business Analytics

Mike Boland, MBA

Would you use data you were never meant to see?


Today’s question

How do we make a defensible decision when data creates value and creates risk for someone else?

Open by asking students for a fast yes/no reaction. Do not define ethics yet. Ask what fact would be most likely to change their answer.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 1.

Ethics is a relationship


People represented

Provide data, behavior, or experiences.

Analysts and researchers

Choose what to collect, combine, model, and report.

Decision makers

Act on the result and distribute its consequences.


Every analytical choice changes at least one of these relationships.

Connect “respondents” from the old deck to the broader category of people represented in administrative, transactional, sensor, and platform data.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 2.

Consent defines the relationship


Meaningful consent
  • understandable purpose;
  • realistic choice;
  • known uses and recipients;
  • proportionate risks; and
  • a way to ask questions or withdraw when feasible.
A checkbox is not enough when…
  • the new use is unexpected;
  • refusal carries a hidden penalty;
  • data will be combined or inferred;
  • consequences reach beyond the original service; or
  • the explanation is technically true but practically unreadable.

Prime directive

Permission for one purpose is not automatic permission for every profitable purpose.

Ask students to distinguish legal acceptance of terms from meaningful, informed consent. The course goal is recognizing the ethical question, not providing legal advice.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 2.

Test the decision through six lenses


Lens Ask
Purpose What problem are we solving, and is this use necessary?
Permission Would the people represented reasonably expect this use?
Privacy What could be revealed, linked, or reidentified?
Fairness Who benefits, who bears risk, and who could be excluded?
Transparency Can we explain the data, method, limitations, and decision?
Accountability Who can stop, correct, appeal, or repair a harmful outcome?

Use these six lenses for every scenario. Students should learn that an ethics review is a structured analysis, not a search for a magic yes/no rule.

Four decisions

Who gets to reuse driving data?

CONNECTED VEHICLE DATA

An automaker records speed, braking, acceleration, and other driving signals. An insurer offers to buy access so it can price policies and investigate disputed claims.

Should the automaker sell the data to the insurer?

Ask groups to identify the original purpose, the proposed secondary use, affected parties, and one condition that would change their decision.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 4.

Secondary use changes the bargain


Potential value
  • better evidence about a claim;
  • pricing that reflects measured behavior;
  • incentives for safer driving; and
  • faster fraud investigation.
Potential harm
  • unexpected surveillance;
  • inaccurate or context-free signals;
  • higher prices without meaningful appeal; and
  • exposure of passengers or household members who never agreed.

Before deciding

Specify consent, data minimization, retention, validation, access controls, allowed uses, and an appeal process.

Emphasize that a plausible benefit does not erase governance requirements. Ask whether the same data should be available for claim investigation and routine pricing.

[Sources] - Scenario adapted from legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 4.

Can a company reveal a pseudonymous user?

IDENTITY INFERENCE

A messaging user chooses a pseudonym. Other users have stored the person’s real name in their contacts, allowing the platform to infer the identity and attach additional demographic information.

Should the platform uncover and use the person’s real identity?

Ask whether a real-name clause in the terms of service resolves the ethical problem. Then ask what the user’s behavior communicates about their expectations.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 5.

De-identified does not mean harmless


Direct identifiers removed

↓

Records linked with contacts, location, behavior, or public information

↓

A person or household may become recognizable again

Expectation gap

What a system can infer may exceed what a person believed they disclosed.

Separate pseudonymity from anonymity. Ask which combinations of otherwise ordinary fields could make a person recognizable.

[Sources] - Scenario adapted from legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 5.

Access does not create permission

CONFLICT OF INTEREST

An intern can edit automated risk tags used by colleges. The intern discovers unfavorable tags attached to a family member’s profile and has valid credentials to change them.

Should the intern alter the family member’s records?

The original scenario used a younger brother and named example tags. The replacement keeps the conflict but removes distracting details.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 6.

The ethical action fixes the system


The intern should not secretly alter one record.

Instead:

  1. preserve the audit trail;
  2. disclose the conflict of interest;
  3. use the documented correction or appeal process;
  4. test whether similar tags are inaccurate for other people; and
  5. escalate a systemic problem rather than creating a private exception.

Access versus authority

Technical capability answers “Can I?” Governance answers “Am I authorized, accountable, and acting consistently?”

Connect this to database credentials, shared Posit projects, grading data, and any other environment where permission is broader than the legitimate purpose.

[Sources] - Scenario adapted from legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 6.

A helpful model can still cause harm

HEALTHCARE PAYMENT RISK

A healthcare organization predicts who may have trouble paying a bill. The score could connect patients with assistance, but it uses demographic and medical factors and may also affect access before services occur.

Should the organization use the model?

Do not let students answer only by pointing to good intentions. Ask them to distinguish a supportive intervention from a punitive one.

[Sources] - Legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 7.

The same score can support or punish


Supportive use Punitive use
Offer financial counseling Delay or deny care
Simplify payment options Increase scrutiny without explanation
Connect people with assistance Use health information unrelated to ability to pay
Let staff review context Treat the score as an unquestionable fact

The model is only part of the system

Evaluate the outcome, training data, error rates, affected groups, intervention, human review, and appeal process together.

Ask who experiences false positives and false negatives. A model can be statistically accurate overall and still create unacceptable consequences for a subgroup or use case.

[Sources] - Scenario adapted from legacy D14_Slides.pptx, slide 7.

Make the reasoning visible

Write an ethical decision record


  1. Purpose: What decision or benefit justifies the analysis?
  2. People: Who is represented, affected, excluded, or able to object?
  3. Data: What was collected, inferred, joined, and retained?
  4. Evidence: How well does the method work, and for whom?
  5. Risks: What foreseeable misuse, error, or unequal consequence exists?
  6. Controls: What consent, minimization, review, security, and appeal are required?
  7. Decision: Proceed, redesign, restrict, or stop - and who owns that choice?

This converts the old closing checklist into a record that can be reviewed later. Point out that ethical analysis should be reproducible just like statistical analysis.

Defend one decision


Choose one scenario and prepare:

  • a decision: proceed, redesign, restrict, or stop;
  • the two strongest reasons supporting it;
  • the person most at risk if you are wrong;
  • one control or safeguard you would require; and
  • one new fact that could change your answer.

Your output

A 60-second recommendation that a decision maker and an affected person could both understand.

Give small groups a scenario. After reports, ask another group to identify one missing stakeholder or consequence.

Can you defend the decision to the people affected?


Good analysis is not only accurate.

It is purposeful, proportionate, explainable, contestable, and accountable.

Return to the opening question. Ask students which lens changed their first reaction and why.
