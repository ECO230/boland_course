MGT 308 Smoke-Test Report

Looking at Consequences of Job Satisfaction at Amazon

Author

OpenAI Codex Smoke Test

1 Executive Summary

This report applies an evidence-based decision-making framework to Amazon-style warehouse work using three kinds of evidence: stakeholder evidence from the assigned PBS Frontline video segment, scientific evidence from peer-reviewed organizational behavior research, and practitioner evidence from HR and management publications. I focus on job satisfaction as the core exogenous variable and test relationships with job performance, support for unionization, and accidents. The central claim is that visible problems such as accidents, disengagement, and pro-union sentiment may reflect a deeper job satisfaction problem rather than isolated operational issues.

Using the provided hypothetical dataset, job satisfaction is positively related to supervisor-rated performance and negatively related to support for unionization, accidents, absences, and turnover. A small path-style model suggests that job satisfaction predicts higher performance, job satisfaction predicts lower union support, and performance helps explain why job satisfaction is related to accidents. In practical terms, this implies that improving the daily work experience should matter not only for morale, but also for safety, behavior, and labor relations.

2 Ask: Core Problem and Purpose

The visible problems in the Amazon case are worker frustration, fear of injury, emotional exhaustion, high pressure, distrust of management, and openness to unionization. The broader organizational behavior problem appears to be a work system that combines high surveillance, strong pace pressure, and limited dignity or control. If that interpretation is correct, then absenteeism, accidents, weaker extra-role behavior, and union support are consequences of a deeper attitudinal problem.

The purpose of this report is to evaluate that interpretation by integrating stakeholder evidence, scientific evidence, practitioner evidence, and the provided dataset, then recommending evidence-based actions leaders could test.

3 Acquire: Stakeholder Evidence

The assigned PBS Frontline segment, “You’re Just Disposable”: Former Amazon Workers Speak Out, presents warehouse work as tightly monitored, quota-driven, and emotionally dehumanizing. Even without heavy quotation, the segment points to a stable pattern:

  • workers describe feeling interchangeable rather than valued;
  • monitoring and output expectations shape the pace of work;
  • workers connect the pace of work to fatigue and injury concerns;
  • low trust in management increases interest in collective employee voice.

Taken together, the stakeholder evidence suggests a plausible causal chain: if workers feel disposable, job satisfaction should decline; if job satisfaction declines, performance quality and discretionary effort may also fall; and if work feels both unfair and unsafe, support for unionization should rise.

4 Acquire: Scientific Literature Review

I selected job satisfaction as the exogenous variable because it is well-supported in the literature and fits the stakeholder evidence closely.

Harrison, Newman, and Roth (2006) show in a major meta-analysis that job attitudes are meaningfully related to outcomes such as performance, absenteeism, turnover, and citizenship-related behavior. This is important because it supports the idea that one underlying attitudinal problem can generate several downstream organizational problems at once.

Riketta (2008) strengthens the directional argument by showing in a meta-analysis of panel studies that job attitudes predict later performance more strongly than performance predicts later attitudes. That makes it more defensible to treat job satisfaction as a starting point in the model rather than only a reaction to outcomes.

Judge and Kammeyer-Mueller (2012) summarize job attitudes research and argue that employee attitudes matter because they shape attachment, motivation, and withdrawal. Their review helps explain why low job satisfaction should be connected to weaker effort, lower trust, and stronger desire to exit or resist the employment relationship.

Judge, Weiss, Kammeyer-Mueller, and Hulin (2017) further argue that job satisfaction remains central because it reflects employees’ broader evaluation of the total work experience. In an Amazon-like environment, that matters because the problem is likely cumulative rather than isolated to one policy.

5 Acquire: Practitioner Evidence

Practitioner-oriented sources generally point in the same direction as the scientific literature.

Gallup argues that low engagement and low satisfaction are expensive because they are associated with lower productivity, higher stress, and weaker retention. Although Gallup uses the term engagement more often than job satisfaction, the practical implication is similar: employee attitudes carry business consequences.

Harvard Business Review has emphasized that employee engagement improves when organizations provide clarity, support, and a sense that leaders care about employee experience. This directly contrasts with the Amazon stakeholder evidence, where workers describe a system focused heavily on control and output.

SHRM’s coverage of toxic workplace culture likewise highlights that organizations pay a real price when employees experience work as disrespectful, unsafe, or psychologically draining. In that environment, turnover risk and interest in collective protection become more understandable.

6 Appraise: Hypotheses and Model

Based on the literature and stakeholder evidence, I tested the following hypotheses:

H1. Higher job satisfaction will be associated with higher supervisor-rated job performance.

H2. Higher job satisfaction will be associated with lower support for unionization.

H3. Higher job satisfaction will be associated with fewer accidents partly through better job performance.

This is intentionally different from the instructor’s work-rates example. I use job satisfaction as the exogenous variable because it better fits the literature base and still speaks directly to the worker experience described in the video.

7 Aggregate: Methods

7.1 Participants

The assignment instructions describe 60 current and former Amazon warehouse employees, along with corresponding supervisor ratings.

7.2 Procedure

Employees completed measures of job satisfaction, affective commitment, and support for unionization. Supervisors rated performance and organizational citizenship behaviors. HR records supplied absences, turnover, productivity rates, and accidents.

7.3 Measures Used in This Report

  • JS: Job Satisfaction
  • Perf: Job Performance
  • Union: Support for Unionization
  • Accidents: Number of accidents per quarter

9 Apply: Discussion and Recommendations

The stakeholder evidence, scientific literature, and dataset all point in a similar direction. Workers describe pressure, surveillance, and low dignity. The research literature says job attitudes predict consequential outcomes. The dataset shows that lower job satisfaction is associated with lower performance, higher union support, more accidents, more absences, and more turnover.

That does not prove that job satisfaction is the only cause. However, it does support treating job satisfaction as a serious operational indicator rather than a cosmetic morale variable. If leaders improve the work experience, they should expect effects that extend beyond attitude into performance, safety, and labor relations.

9.1 Recommendations

  1. Redesign work pace around sustainable performance.
    Leaders should audit whether quotas and monitoring systems create chronic fatigue or unsafe shortcuts.

  2. Increase employee voice and procedural fairness.
    Workers need credible ways to raise concerns about pace, safety, and treatment without retaliation.

  3. Treat job satisfaction as an operating metric.
    Satisfaction should be reviewed alongside throughput, defects, safety incidents, and turnover.

  4. Target safety through system design.
    Because accidents are tied to performance and satisfaction, leaders should not frame safety solely as an individual compliance issue.

9.2 Evaluation Plan

Amazon should evaluate any intervention using pre/post metrics on:

  • job satisfaction;
  • absences;
  • accidents;
  • turnover;
  • support for unionization;
  • performance ratings.

The best evaluation design would be a pilot comparison between intervention sites and matched control sites.

10 Limitations and Future Research

This smoke-test report has several limitations:

  • the dataset is hypothetical;
  • the 60-row file appears to duplicate the first 30 rows;
  • several measures are single-item indicators;
  • the design is correlational rather than experimental;
  • the stakeholder evidence is strong on work pace and surveillance, but this report chose job satisfaction as the exogenous variable to align with the strongest literature base.

Future work could test a broader model in which Rates predicts JS, and JS then predicts accidents, absenteeism, turnover, and union support.
